0010 · Characterizing System Performance

EE track · 6.01SC Ch.5 (step response) · ~30 minutes · mission · github.io

Pole locations told you whether a loop converges. This lesson tells you how well it converges — three numbers read off the step response that let you compare two controllers without re-running the simulation.

Why this matters for your mission. A BESS charge controller that reaches its setpoint in 2 seconds but overshoots into an overvoltage trip is worse than one that takes 5 seconds cleanly. Rise time, overshoot, and settling time are the acceptance criteria you would write into a vendor spec — and the numbers you check first when a loop "works but hunts".

1 · The step response and its landmarks

Apply a unit step input (0 → 1 at n = 0) and record the output y[n]. Three landmarks on that curve:

  • Rise time — how fast it first climbs toward the target.
  • Overshoot — how far it shoots past the target.
  • Settling time — when it finally stays near the target.

Conventions: rise = 10% → 90% of final value; overshoot = peak excess as % of final; settling = last entry into a ±5% (sometimes ±2%) band around final.

2 · Rise time: speed of first approach

Rise time measures the initial climb only — it says nothing about what happens after. A fast rise with heavy ringing can settle later than a slow clean rise.

y[n] = 1 − 0.8ⁿ        (first-order approach to 1)
y[0]=0, y[1]=0.20, y[2]=0.36, y[4]=0.59, y[7]=0.79, y[10]=0.89
10% crossed at n=1, 90% near n=11 → rise ≈ 10 steps

3 · Overshoot: the price of speed

Overshoot appears when complex pole pairs (or aggressive gains) push the response past target before the feedback can reverse it:

final value 1.00, peak 1.25 → overshoot = (1.25 − 1.00)/1.00 = 25%

Rule of thumb: raising loop gain shortens rise time but grows overshoot — the central trade you tune in 0011.

4 · Settling time and steady-state error

  • Settling time: smallest N such that |y[n] − y[∞]| ≤ 0.05·y[∞] for all n ≥ N.
  • Steady-state error: 1 − y[∞] for a unit step — what proportional control alone can never fully kill (see 0011).

For a dominant pole p, settling ≈ time for |p|ⁿ to fall below 5%: n ≈ ln(0.05)/ln|p|. With p = 0.8: ln(0.05)/ln(0.8) ≈ 13.4 → settles ~n = 14.

5 · Worked check: two tunings compared

Same plant, two gains, unit step:

  • Tuning A: rise 3 steps, overshoot 30%, settles n = 22.
  • Tuning B: rise 8 steps, overshoot 2%, settles n = 12.

B wins on settling despite slower rise — the ringing in A costs 10 extra steps. Report all three numbers together; any single one misleads.

Quiz

  1. What does rise time quantify?

    • Time for response to climb
    • Time for overshoot to vanish
    • Time for input to start
    • Time for gain to settle

    Rise time is the 10%→90% climb interval — speed of first approach, nothing about ringing after.

  2. What does percent overshoot express?

    • Peak delay before response starts
    • Steady error after long time
    • Peak excess beyond final value
    • Settling band around final value

    Overshoot = (peak − final)/final as % — how far the response shoots past target.

  3. What defines settling time?

    • First instant response leaves zero
    • Last entry into error band
    • Peak moment of maximum overshoot
    • Initial slope of step response

    Settling = last time the response enters and stays in the ±5% band around final value.

  4. Why specify all three metrics together?

    • Speed alone guarantees zero overshoot
    • Overshoot alone fixes steady error
    • Settling alone sets rise speed
    • Speed ringing and precision conflict

    Faster rise costs overshoot; overshoot costs settling. Only the trio describes the tradeoff.
