Refrigeration Cycle & COP

An industrial heat pump is a refrigerator pointed the wrong way: instead of using work to move heat out of a cold box, it uses work to lift heat from a warm waste stream into a hot process loop. This lesson gives you the 4-stage cycle, the one definition the whole engagement turns on (COP), and the ideal-vs-actual gap that explains the finding you will verify hands-on in lesson 0008: ideal COP 6.22 vs. actual COP 3.11 on the 45→95°C dye-house case.

45–60 min · recall first, then check · mission brief

Mission link

Every number in the breakeven-COP table (0007) and every REopt result (0011) is downstream of this lesson's definition: COP = Q_heat_delivered / W_electrical_input. A heat pump with COP 3.11 delivers 3.11 kWh of heat per kWh of electricity — that is why it can compete with burning coal, but only at the right hours of the day.

01The four stages of the vapor-compression cycle

  1. Evaporator (low pressure) — liquid refrigerant boils, absorbing heat from the source. In the dye-house case the source is 45°C wastewater; the refrigerant must boil below 45°C to absorb from it.
  2. Compressor — electrical work compresses the vapour to high pressure, raising its saturation temperature far above the sink temperature. This is where the electricity bill is incurred — and the only place work enters the cycle.
  3. Condenser (high pressure) — the hot vapour condenses, delivering its latent heat (lesson 0001!) to the sink: the 95°C hot-water loop.
  4. Expansion valve — pressure drops, part of the liquid flashes (self-cooling), and the cycle repeats. No work recovered in a simple cycle.

The refrigerant is just an energy taxi: R1234ze(Z) is what heat_pump_model's default run uses because its pressure–temperature curve fits a ~95°C sink without extreme pressures.

02COP defined — and why it isn't "efficiency"

COP = Q_heat_delivered / W_electrical_input

A resistance heater has COP = 1.0 exactly — one kWh of electricity becomes one kWh of heat. That is the floor, not the benchmark. A heat pump with COP 3.11 moves 3.11 kWh of heat per kWh bought: 2.11 kWh are free, lifted from the waste stream. That is the entire economic argument for industrial heat pumps, and the reason REopt's placeholder ElectricHeater (cop 1.0) loses to coal at every hour while a COP 3.11 machine wins most hours (lesson 0007).

Note COP is a ratio of energies, not a percentage — a COP of 3.11 does not mean "311% efficient"; it means more heat moved than work spent, paid for by the temperature lift.

03Ideal vs. actual COP — second-law efficiency

The Carnot (ideal) COP for a heat pump lifting between source temperature T_source and sink temperature T_sink (absolute, Kelvin):

COP_ideal = T_sink / (T_sink − T_source)

COP_actual = η_II × COP_ideal

No real machine reaches the ideal — compressor losses, pressure drops, finite heat-exchanger area, superheat/subcooling compromises. Real machines are characterised by their second-law efficiency, η_II — the fraction of the ideal performance actually achieved.

Two instincts worth building: (1) smaller lifts → much better COP — halving the lift roughly doubles the ideal COP; (2) η_II is the engineering reality knob — vendors quote machines between roughly 40% and 60% second-law efficiency, and that spread can decide whether a project clears the breakeven bar at all.

QPractice quiz

6 questions · answer to reveal reasoningScore 0 / 6

  1. In which component does the cycle absorb heat from the waste-water source?

    Why — The evaporator boils refrigerant below the source temperature so heat flows in from the 45°C wastewater. The condenser rejects/delivers at the hot side; the compressor consumes the work.

  2. What is the definition of a heat pump's COP?

    Why — COP = Q_heat_delivered / W_electrical_input. It measures how much useful heat each kWh of electricity buys — the denominator is work, never fuel or temperature.

  3. A plain resistance heater (like REopt's cop-1.0 ElectricHeater) has what COP?

    Why — Resistance heat converts every kWh bought into 1 kWh of heat — COP 1.0. It cannot exceed 1 because no heat is moved from anywhere. That is why it loses to coal steam at every hour of the Vietnamese tariff, while a COP 3.11 heat pump does not.

  4. Using COP_actual = η_II × COP_ideal: with ideal COP 6.22 and second-law efficiency 50%, what actual COP results?

    Why — 0.5 × 6.22 = 3.11 — matching ee-heat's corroborated dye-house finding exactly, which is the number the capstone scenario feeds to ASHPWaterHeater as its COP.

  5. For a fixed sink temperature, what happens to the ideal COP when the source gets colder (larger lift)?

    Why — Bigger lift ⇒ bigger denominator ⇒ smaller COP. Halving the lift roughly doubles the ideal COP, which is precisely why the low-lift 45→95°C case is attractive and why making high-pressure steam with a heat pump is hard.

  6. Why is a COP of 3.11 not described as "311% efficient"?

    Why — First law holds: 3.11 kWh out = 1 kWh work + 2.11 kWh lifted from the waste stream. Nothing is created. Calling it 311% invites exactly the wrong mental model — the second-law accounting lives in η_II, which here is 50%.
