Steam Systems Fundamentals

The workplan's clean-heat priority (i) is steam optimization and waste heat recovery — the load-reduction layer that comes before any heat pump. This lesson walks the five things to check, in the exact order ee-heat's VN settings card prescribes: condensate return → blowdown & heat recovery → flash steam → stack losses/excess air → distribution insulation. That ordering is not arbitrary; it runs roughly largest and cheapest savings first.

45–60 min · recall first, then check · mission brief

Mission link

The tool-ordering rule — MEASUR first (shrink the load), REopt second (size the replacement) — is this lesson in two words. Sizing a heat pump against an unoptimized steam load buys ~30% too much equipment and produces a wrong LCOH. These five checks are what MEASUR's Steam System Modeler quantifies, one by one.

01Boiler efficiency: fuel in vs. steam out

η_boiler = useful heat delivered in steam / fuel energy burned

The engagement's placeholder plant runs an existing coal boiler at 75% "as found". Every unit of loss between fuel and steam is money up the stack or down the drain. Two consequences you will use constantly:

  • Coal at $5.30/MMBtu fuel is really $5.30 / 0.75 = $7.07 per MMBtu of useful delivered heat. That $7.07/MMBtu is the number the whole breakeven-COP table compares against.
  • A boiler-efficiency improvement (excess-air trim, cleaner firesides) shrinks the load the heat pump must serve and worsens the heat pump's relative economics — which is why the sequencing rule exists.

02The five checks, in order

1 Condensate return rate biggest · cheapest win

After steam gives up its latent heat it condenses into hot water — condensate — still carrying sensible heat (lesson 0001). Returning it to the feedwater tank instead of dumping it saves three ways at once: fuel (don't re-heat cold make-up), water-treatment chemicals (make-up needs softening/deaeration), and water itself. First on the list because it is usually the biggest, cheapest win in a garment plant.

2 Blowdown rate & blowdown heat recovery purge ≠ waste

Boiler water concentrates dissolved solids as steam leaves; excess solids are purged = blowdown — hot water straight to drain. Too little and the boiler foams/carryovers; too much and you dump expensive hot treated water continuously. Recovery: a blowdown flash tank + heat exchanger preheats make-up with the purge stream. MEASUR computes the optimal rate from measured conductivity.

3 Flash steam recovery free low-pressure steam

High-pressure condensate, when dropped to lower pressure, is superheated relative to the new saturation point — part of it instantly re-boils as flash steam. Venting flash tanks to atmosphere vent exactly that. Recover it (into the deaerator or a low-pressure header) and you get free steam. Rule of thumb from lesson 0001's enthalpy bookkeeping: the fraction flashed is the sensible-heat surplus divided by the latent heat at the lower pressure — typically 10–20% per pressure-drop stage.

4 Stack losses / excess air combustion-side tuning

The biggest single boiler loss is usually hot flue gas leaving the stack. Burners run with excess air for safety, but every point of excess O₂ carries useful heat out. Trim excess air, add an economizer that feeds make-up water with stack heat. This is combustion-side tuning — the cheapest efficiency there is.

5 Distribution insulation unglamorous · pays back in months

Bare or damaged insulation on headers, valves, flanges radiates and conducts heat away (lesson 0001's mechanisms 1 and 3) across the whole 16 h/day operating window. A survey with a thermal camera plus steamed-up insulation-repair job is unglamorous and pays back in months. This closes the list — smallest/last not because it doesn't matter but because the earlier items usually dominate.

Worked example — why condensate return dominates

Placeholder plant: 60,000 MMBtu/yr process heat, coal at $5.30/MMBtu fuel.

Case A — all condensate dumped: feedwater arrives at ~27°C after treatment.
Case B — 80% condensate return: feedwater averages ~70°C.

Extra sensible duty per kg of make-up water (ΔT = 43°C):
  4.186 kJ/kg·°C × 43°C ≈ 180 kJ/kg   ← pure extra boiler duty in Case A

At η = 0.75, fuel cost of heating make-up alone rises ~$0.5–1/MMBtu-equivalent on the
load — before counting water, chemical, and blowdown savings. Multiply by thousands of
hours: this is why return rate is Check #1.

QPractice quiz

6 questions · answer to reveal reasoningScore 0 / 6

  1. Returning condensate instead of dumping it saves which combination?

    Why — Hot returned condensate means less re-heating (fuel), less cold make-up to soften/deaerate (chemicals), and less purchased water. Three savings in one measure — hence Check #1.

  2. What is blowdown?

    Why — Steam leaves impurities behind; the concentration must be capped by purging hot treated water. Blowdown heat recovery turns that purge from a loss into make-up preheating.

  3. Flash steam forms when…

    Why — Lower-pressure saturation temperature is lower; the condensate carries sensible heat above it, so the surplus boils a fraction off as flash steam. Venting it wastes free low-pressure steam.

  4. With fuel at $5.30/MMBtu and boiler efficiency 75%, the cost of useful delivered heat is…

    Why — Only three-quarters of the fuel's heat becomes useful steam, so each useful MMBtu costs 1/0.75 of the fuel price: $7.07/MMBtu. This is exactly the denominator value in the breakeven-COP formula used in lessons 0007 and 0011.

  5. Why does the checklist put stack/excess-air tuning fourth and insulation last, despite both being real losses?

    Why — The card's ordering encodes expected impact-per-effort. Stack trimming and insulation remain worthwhile — they are simply usually smaller than the top three in this sector.

  6. Per the tool-ordering rule, what happens if you skip steam optimization and hand REopt today's raw load?

    Why — REopt optimizes economics over whatever load it is given; it does not know the load could shrink. Garbage-in sizing: roughly 30% over-buy and a misleading LCOH — the exact failure mode activeContext.md warns about.
