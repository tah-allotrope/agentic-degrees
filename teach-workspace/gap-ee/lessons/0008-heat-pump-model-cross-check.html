Set/confirm the case parameters: source 45°C (dye-house waste water), sink 95°C (hot-water loop), refrigerant R1234ze(Z) (the model's default), second-law efficiency 50%. Run the notebook top to bottom and read out:

  • Ideal COP ≈ 6.22 — the model's cycle-level ideal on real refrigerant properties (pure Carnot would be ~7.36 at this lift; lesson 0002 explains the gap).

  • Dropped onto the tariff (lesson 0007): $4.27/MMBtu off-peak (−40%), $6.58 normal (−7%), $12.20 peak (+73%). Same conclusion as REopt via completely different machinery: charge off-peak, ride through peak — heat pump + thermal storage.

3. Why the cost side of this tool is radioactive

The same notebook also prints techno-economics. Do not use them:

  • It reports a negative capital cost — physically absurd.
  • Its operating-cost units come out garbled ($·MMBtu/h/kW/yr-shaped).
  • Its headline LCOH ($2.24/MMBtu) is below what the electricity ALONE costs at COP 3.11 ($6.58/MMBtu normal-hours) — an LCOH below its own energy floor cannot be right.

Diagnosis: the repo is stale (last commit Nov 2022), undocumented, unlicensed, and its cost module was built for US assumptions nobody has revalidated. The COP side rests on CoolProp — a maintained, peer-used property library — so it survives scrutiny. Rule, verbatim from tools/README.md: "Use this model for COP, take costs from REopt." That is also why it ranks #3 "reference only" in activeContext.md's table.

  2. What makes this model's COP outputs trustworthy while its cost outputs are not?

    • COP numbers are smaller and therefore less error-prone
    • COP rests on CoolProp's maintained refrigerant-property calculations; the cost module is stale, undocumented, and produces physically impossible values
    • Costs are unreliable only outside the US
    • Trustworthiness depends on the refrigerant chosen

    Different modules, different provenance: physics from CoolProp (sound); economics from a stale hand-rolled module (negative capex, garbled units, sub-energy-floor LCOH). Audit components, not tools.

  4. The tool prints LCOH $2.24/MMBtu for a machine using electricity worth $6.58/MMBtu of heat at COP 3.11. What follows?

    • The LCOH is wrong by construction — total cost can't sit below the energy-input cost alone
    • The electricity price must be wrong instead
    • Storage could bring delivered heat below its own energy cost
    • Nothing — LCOH routinely sits below energy cost for efficient plants

    LCOH ≥ energy cost per MMBtu, always. A sub-floor LCOH is proof of a broken cost chain, independent of any other evidence — which is precisely what the negative-capital and garbled-units symptoms corroborate.

  5. Why run the notebook's case at 45→95°C specifically?

    • It is the model's shipped default case
    • Because heat pumps cannot exceed 95°C under any circumstances
    • It mirrors the plant's real dye-house opportunity — waste-water source to hot-water sink, inside clean-heat priority band (ii), <100–110°C
    • Because R1234ze(Z) only works below 100°C by regulation

    The engagement targets low-temp process heat: 45°C wastewater is a free heat source going down the drain, and the 60–95°C loop is where a heat pump competes. The case is chosen by plant reality, not tool convenience.
