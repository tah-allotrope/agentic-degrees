0005 · TEA & LCOH Fundamentals

gap-ee week 1, day 5 · 45-60 min · recall first, then check · mission

Week 1 closes with the economics vocabulary that makes REopt's output readable: net present value (NPV), payback, and levelized cost of heat (LCOH) — the $/MMBtu figure the whole engagement compares against coal. One worked example carries the lesson: two candidate heat sources, different capital costs, lifetimes, and outputs — which one actually delivers cheaper heat?

Mission link. activeContext.md's sequencing rule is an LCOH statement: "sizing a heat pump against an unoptimized steam load over-sizes the equipment and produces a wrong LCOH." After this lesson you can say precisely why: capital dominates LCOH, so an over-sized machine poisons every downstream number REopt prints.

1. Discounting and NPV in one paragraph

A dollar today and a dollar in year 15 are not the same dollar. Discounting divides each future year's cash flow by (1 + r)^n, where r is the discount rate (REopt scenarios use an offtaker rate of 10%) and n the year. Net present value sums all discounted cash flows — capital outlay (negative), fuel, electricity, O&M (negative), any savings or revenues (positive). NPV > 0 means the project creates value at that discount rate. When REopt reports "NPV $0" for an option it declined to build, it means: building it would not improve on doing nothing.

2. Simple payback

payback_years = capital_cost / annual_net_savings

Crude but universal on factory floors. It ignores the time value of money entirely (see §1), ignores anything after payback year, and flatters short-lived equipment. Use it to communicate, not to decide — decisions belong to NPV/LCOH.

3. LCOH — the engagement's headline metric

LCOH = total lifecycle cost of the heat source / total lifetime heat delivered   [$/MMBtu]

Sum discounted capital + fixed O&M over the lifetime; add discounted fuel/electricity + variable O&M as delivered; divide by discounted heat delivered. It converts wildly different technologies (a boiler burning coal vs. a heat pump eating electricity vs. solar thermal with a big upfront cost) into one comparable number per MMBtu — which is exactly what lesson 0007's breakeven table needs.

Worked example — two candidate systems, one load

Serve 60,000 MMBtu/yr useful heat. Discount rate 10%, 20 years.
Annuity factor A = (1 − 1.1^−20)/0.10 ≈ 8.514   (present value of $1/yr for 20 yr)

System H — industrial heat pump
  Capital:        $2,400,000          → annualised capex = 2,400,000 / 8.514 ≈ $281,900/yr
  Fixed O&M:      $24,000/yr
  Electricity:    COP 3.11 → 60,000 MMBtu ÷ 293.071 kWh/MMBtu × ... see below
    heat energy   = 60,000 MMBtu × 293.071 kWh/MMBtu = 17,584,260 kWh_th
    electricity   = 17,584,260 / 3.11 ≈ 5,653,460 kWh_e
    cost @ blended ~$0.06983/kWh (normal-rate case)
                  ≈ 5,653,460 × 0.06983 ≈ $394,800/yr

System B — new coal boiler (same 75% efficiency)
  Capital:        $600,000            → annualised capex = 600,000 / 8.514 ≈ $70,500/yr
  Fixed O&M:      $12,000/yr
  Coal:           60,000 / 0.75 = 80,000 MMBtu fuel × $5.30 ≈ $424,000/yr

LCOH_H = (281,900 + 24,000 + 394,800) / 60,000 = 700,700 / 60,000 ≈ $11.68/MMBtu
LCOH_B = (70,500 + 12,000 + 424,000)  / 60,000 = 506,500 / 60,000 ≈ $8.44/MMBtu

Read it carefully — this is the honest version of the comparison:

  • At the blended normal-hours rate, the heat pump's delivered heat costs about 394,800/60,000 ≈ $6.58/MMBtu in energy alone (matching ee-heat's published $6.58 normal-hours figure for COP 3.11), but its capital drags total LCOH above the coal boiler's.
  • The lever that flips the answer is when the electricity is bought — off-peak at $0.04533/kWh cuts the energy term ~35%, and thermal storage is the mechanism to buy off-peak heat and serve peak demand. That design question is exactly what the capstone (0011) asks REopt to optimize, hour by hour, instead of trusting a single blended rate like this example does.
  • Notice how much of LCOH_H is capital ($305,900 of $700,700). Over-size the heat pump by serving an unoptimized load and LCOH_H inflates almost linearly — the sequencing rule from lesson 0004, now with numbers attached.

Practice quiz

  1. What does an NPV of exactly $0 mean in REopt's output?

    • The scenario failed to solve
    • The project pays back in exactly half its lifetime
    • Building the option neither creates nor destroys value vs. doing nothing — typically why REopt declines to build it
    • Fuel prices were escalated to zero

    Baseline run (Phase 3) shows "Electric heater … NPV $0": at COP 1.0 the electric boiler cannot beat coal, so its optimal size is zero. NPV $0 = no improvement over BAU.

  2. Simple payback ignores which major factor?

    • The time value of money (discounting)
    • Capital cost
    • Annual savings
    • Equipment location

    Payback = capital ÷ annual savings, undiscounted, and blind to everything after the payback year. Communicate with it; decide with NPV/LCOH.

  3. LCOH is best described as…

    • The first-year fuel bill divided by nameplate capacity
    • Capital cost divided by annual heat output
    • Total lifecycle cost divided by total lifetime heat delivered — a $/MMBtu figure comparable across technologies
    • The tariff rate charged by the utility per MMBtu of steam

    LCOH puts boiler, heat pump, and solar thermal on one axis: discounted (capital + O&M + energy) ÷ discounted heat delivered. That comparability is why the engagement quotes coal at $7.07/MMBtu delivered.

  4. In the worked example, most of System H's LCOH is…

    • Fixed O&M
    • Electricity
    • Annualised capital (~$306K of ~$701K per year)
    • Coal

    Capital dominates the heat pump's LCOH. Consequence: sizing against an unoptimized (too-big) load inflates LCOH nearly linearly — the quantitative form of the MEASUR-before-REopt rule.

  5. Why does the lesson still call the heat pump promising despite LCOH_H > LCOH_B?

    • Because COP will automatically rise after installation
    • Because the example forgot to include the boiler's emissions
    • Because its energy term was priced at one blended rate — buying off-peak power (with thermal storage) cuts the energy term ~35% and changes the verdict
    • Because LCOH never applies to heat pumps

    The blended-rate example mirrors the "single breakeven ~3.3" trap of lesson 0007: hour-by-hour pricing plus storage is the real design space, and optimizing exactly that is the capstone's job.

  6. Sizing a heat pump against today's unoptimized steam load primarily corrupts which quantity?

    • The grid emission factor
    • The tariff windows
    • LCOH — an over-sized machine adds capital (and part-load losses) that inflates lifetime cost per MMBtu
    • The FX conversion

    activeContext.md's warning verbatim: over-sizing produces "a wrong LCOH." Shrink the load first (MEASUR), then size (REopt).
