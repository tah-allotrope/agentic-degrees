0001 · Thermo & Heat-Transfer Primer

gap-ee week 1, day 1 · 45-60 min · recall first, then check · mission

Everything in the clean-heat toolchain is thermodynamics wearing different clothes: MEASUR balances steam enthalpies, REopt prices heat per MMBtu, heat_pump_model moves heat up a temperature hill. This lesson gives you the three ideas you need before any of those tools make sense — how heat moves, why steam carries so much more energy than hot water, and why MEASUR insists on Metric units.

Mission link. The engagement's core finding compares a heat pump's COP against coal steam economics. You cannot reason about COP (lesson 0002), boiler efficiency (0003), or a $/MMBtu tariff spread (0007) without the unit system and the sensible/latent distinction from this lesson.

1. Three ways heat moves

Heat flows from hot to cold, by exactly three mechanisms:

  • Conduction — through solid material. A boiler shell, a pipe wall, an insulation layer. Governed by Q = k·A·ΔT/L: conductivity k, area A, temperature difference ΔT, thickness L. This is why distribution-line insulation works: fibreglass has ~20× lower k than steel.
  • Convection — heat carried by a moving fluid. Steam condensing on a dyeing machine's jacket, air blowing over a dryer coil. Q = h·A·ΔT. Forced convection (a fan/pump) beats natural convection by 5–10× on h.
  • Radiation — electromagnetic, needs no medium, scales with T⁴. Matters for uninsulated boiler and header surfaces at 100°C+: a bare hot surface radiates and convects away useful fuel money simultaneously.

In plant work you almost never solve these equations — you use tools that do. But you must recognise which mechanism a measure targets: insulation attacks conduction+radiation; a condensate-return pump is about recovering already-transferred heat; excess-air control fixes convection losses up the stack.

2. Sensible vs. latent heat — why steam systems exist

Sensible heat changes a substance's temperature. Latent heat changes its phase at constant temperature. For water:

  • Specific heat: c_p ≈ 4.186 kJ/kg·°C (liquid water)
  • Latent heat of vaporisation at 100°C: h_fg ≈ 2257 kJ/kg

Worked example — heating vs. vaporising 1000 kg of water

Sensible:  1000 kg × 4.186 kJ/kg·°C × (100 − 20)°C = 334,880 kJ
Latent:    1000 kg × 2257 kJ/kg                    = 2,257,000 kJ

Ratio:     2,257,000 / 334,880 ≈ 6.7×

Vaporising the same kilogram costs ~6.7× more energy than heating it from 20°C to boiling. That single ratio explains four things at once:

  1. Why steam is the transport fluid for industrial heat — one kilogram of steam delivers 2257 kJ when it condenses, at constant temperature, inside the machine that needs it.
  2. Why condensate return dominates the savings list in lesson 0003: dump hot condensate and you throw away both its sensible heat and the treated water.
  3. Why dryers and stenters are hungry — they boil water all day (latent duty).
  4. Why a dye-house loop at 60–95°C (hot water, no boiling) is a fundamentally cheaper, lower-temperature target than the steam header — and exactly where a heat pump can win.

3. The unit system: °C / bar(g) / kJ — and why Metric matters here

Plant data in Vietnam arrives in tonnes of steam per hour, bar(g), °C, and kJ. ee-heat/tools/measur/VN-settings-card.md's first instruction is therefore "Set units to Metric" — converting every reading to Imperial just to satisfy a US default is where transcription errors get made.

  • bar(g) = gauge pressure (relative to atmosphere). Steam properties tables key on absolute pressure, but plants speak gauge. 10 bar(g) ≈ 11 bar absolute ≈ 184°C saturation.
  • kJ vs MMBtu — the toolchain speaks both: MEASUR takes kJ/GJ, REopt prices per MMBtu. One conversion to memorise: 1 MMBtu = 293.071 kWh (used everywhere in lessons 0006–0011). Also useful: 1 GJ = 0.9478 MMBtu.
  • The VN settings card prices coal at $5.02/GJ — the same price as the $5.30/MMBtu used in REopt, expressed in the other unit family. Check it: $5.30/MMBtu ÷ 1.055 GJ/MMBtu ≈ $5.02/GJ. Same number, two dialects.

Practice quiz

  1. Which heat-transfer mechanism does adding insulation to a steam distribution line primarily attack?

    • Conduction through the pipe/surface plus surface radiation
    • Convection inside the flowing steam
    • Latent heat of the steam
    • Radiation from the flame inside the burner

    Insulation adds a low-conductivity layer (k ~20× lower than steel) and closes the hot outer surface, cutting conducted-and-radiated losses. The steam side's internal convection is untouched — it was never the loss path.

  2. Using c_p = 4.186 kJ/kg·°C and h_fg = 2257 kJ/kg: raising 1000 kg of water from 20°C to 100°C costs 334,880 kJ. Roughly how much more energy to then vaporise all of it at 100°C?

    • About the same again (~330,000 kJ)
    • About half as much again (~170,000 kJ)
    • About 20× more (~6.7 million kJ)
    • About 6.7× more (~2,257,000 kJ)

    Latent heat is 1000 kg × 2257 kJ/kg = 2,257,000 kJ, and 2,257,000 / 334,880 ≈ 6.7. Vaporising costs several times more than sensible heating — that is why steam carries so much energy per kilogram.

  3. Condensing steam inside a dyeing machine transfers its latent heat at what temperature behaviour?

    • Temperature falls steadily while heat is released
    • Constant temperature until all vapour has condensed
    • Temperature rises steadily while heat is released
    • Temperature oscillates with pressure spikes

    Phase change happens at constant temperature (at fixed pressure). This is exactly what a dye bath wants — heat delivered at a stable, controlled temperature.

  4. MEASUR ships with US/Imperial defaults. The VN settings card says set units to Metric before entering anything. Why?

    • Metric results are more accurate numerically
    • Imperial units are unsupported in MEASUR v1.8.3
    • Vietnamese plant data arrives in tonnes/h, bar(g), °C, kJ — avoiding unit-by-hand conversion avoids transcription errors
    • Metric mode unlocks the waste-heat module only

    The card's exact reasoning: "converting every reading to Imperial to satisfy a default is where transcription errors get made." It is a data-integrity rule, not a precision claim.

  5. The toolchain quotes coal at $5.30/MMBtu (REopt) and $5.02/GJ (MEASUR). Are these consistent? (1 GJ = 0.9478 MMBtu)

    • Yes — $5.30/MMBtu ÷ 1.055 GJ/MMBtu ≈ $5.02/GJ
    • No — one of them is wrong by roughly 40%
    • No — GJ prices must always be larger numbers than MMBtu prices
    • Cannot tell without the boiler efficiency

    A MMBtu is 1.055 GJ, so dividing $5.30 by 1.055 gives $5.02 per GJ. Same price, two unit families — the conversion constant between them is worth memorising because the toolchain switches freely.

  6. Why is the 60–95°C dye-house loop a more promising heat-pump target than the 10 bar(g) steam header?

    • Because hot water stores more latent heat per kg than steam
    • Because heat pumps can only run on hot water, never steam
    • Lifting heat across a small temperature lift (e.g. 45→95°C) needs far less work than making 10 bar(g) steam — higher achievable COP
    • Because the dye-house runs at higher pressure than the header

    COP falls as the temperature lift grows. A 45→95°C lift keeps the ideal COP high (lesson 0002 computes 6.22); producing 10 bar(g) steam would demand a much larger lift and much worse COP. Clean-heat priority (ii) is deliberately the <100–110°C band.
